Why social-science reviews feel harder than they should

Bigger evidence bases. A clinical meta-analysis may pool a handful of trials; an education or psychology review routinely screens thousands of records and includes 50 or more studies. Every manual step — redrawing a flow diagram, reformatting a forest plot, rebuilding a bias table — is multiplied by that scale.

PRISMA pressure is spreading. PRISMA 2020 was written as a general reporting standard, not a medical one, and journals, funders and thesis committees in education and psychology increasingly expect a compliant flow diagram and checklist — often without providing any tooling for it.

Methods are standardising. Registered protocols, dual screening, structured risk-of-bias appraisal and reproducible analysis are becoming the norm outside medicine too. The workflow that medical reviewers built over the last decade transfers almost unchanged — the tools below are that workflow, minus the medical vocabulary.

FAQ

Is this only for medical meta-analyses?

No. The tools were built by a medical researcher, but nothing in them is medicine-specific. PRISMA 2020 is a discipline-neutral reporting standard, a forest plot pools effect sizes the same way whether they come from classroom interventions or clinical trials, and the risk-of-bias plotter accepts judgements from any appraisal tool. Education and psychology reviewers use the same four tools with their own data.

Does PRISMA 2020 apply to education and psychology reviews?

Yes. PRISMA 2020 was written as a general reporting guideline for systematic reviews, not a medical one, and journals and funders in education and psychology increasingly expect a PRISMA-style flow diagram and checklist. The generator on this page produces the standard PRISMA 2020 diagram from your own identification, screening and inclusion counts.

Which risk-of-bias tool should I use — RoB 2, ROBINS-I, or something else?

That depends on your included designs: RoB 2 for randomised trials, ROBINS-I for non-randomised studies of interventions, and discipline-specific standards where your field requires them. The traffic-light generator plots whatever domain judgements you enter and can apply the RoB 2 overall-judgement rule when you use RoB 2; it does not force one appraisal framework on you.

My meta-analysis has more than 50 included studies. Can the tools handle that?

Yes. Large evidence bases are the norm in education and psychology, and the forest plot and traffic-light generators are built for long study lists — paste your study-level results and the plots are drawn in your browser. Because everything runs locally, there is no upload limit and your unpublished data never leaves your machine.

Are the tools really free? Do I need to install anything or create an account?

The four tools are free, with no account, no installation and no watermarks. They run entirely in your browser: open the tool page, enter or paste your numbers, and download the figure. Your data is not uploaded to any server.
